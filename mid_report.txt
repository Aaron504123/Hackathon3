1. What specific problem or pain point is your project addressing?
The main problem we are trying to solve is that disabled people and elderly with limited mobility in Hong Kong often cannot find reliable real-time accessibility information when using public transport. Things like whether an MTR lift is working, if there are steep slopes, or if a bus is low-floor are scattered across different apps and websites. This makes it very hard for them to plan a trip with confidence, and many end up facing unexpected barriers halfway through their journey.
2. What research have you conducted to understand the problem better?
Our problem framing is based on secondary research, including public information, reports about accessibility barriers, and a review of transport and map services. So far, our hands-on validation has focused on whether the prototype can geocode Hong Kong locations, request a walking route, sample terrain elevations, and show nearby facilities. We have not yet completed formal usability testing with wheelchair users or older adults, so that remains an important next step.
3. Why is this problem significant? Who does it affect?
This problem is important because being able to move around independently affects almost every part of life – going to work, seeing the doctor, or meeting friends. When the information is unreliable, disabled people and elderly residents become more isolated. It mainly affects wheelchair users and older people with mobility issues, but it also puts extra pressure on their families and carers. Solving it would help make the city fairer and more inclusive.
4. What existing solutions are available and how do they fall short?
Services such as MTR Care, HKeMobility, and general map apps provide useful transport or navigation information. Our current prototype focuses on a narrower gap: showing terrain slope along a walking route and marking nearby facilities on a map. It does not yet provide live lift or bus status, temporary-obstacle reports, multiple accessible route choices, or a guarantee that a route is wheelchair-accessible.
5. Describe the primary objectives of your project.
The current objective is to demonstrate an end-to-end Hong Kong walking-route prototype: users enter place names, the frontend converts them to coordinates, and the backend returns a Mapbox route with DEM-based slope segments and nearby facility markers. The interface uses colour to make slope levels easier to scan. Multiple route comparisons, user obstacle reporting, live transport status, and a validated wheelchair-accessibility score are future goals, not completed features.
6. What are the boundaries of your project (what will the project not address)?
We decided not to include full offline maps for the whole of Hong Kong in this stage. We are also not linking to paid taxi or rehab bus booking systems, and we are not trying to provide personal care support or real-time seat availability that is not publicly available. These limits help us stay focused and realistic within the time we have.
7. What assumptions are you making about the problem/pain point and its context?
The prototype assumes users have an internet connection and that external map, geocoding, terrain, and facility services are available. It currently uses a Hong Kong coordinate bounding box, which is an approximation rather than an exact territory boundary. We also assume that displaying slope and nearby facilities is useful, but the accuracy and usefulness of those indicators still need to be checked with target users and local route observations.
8. Who is your target audience or user base?
Our main users are wheelchair users and elderly people who have trouble walking. Many of them are not very familiar with complicated apps, and some have difficulty with small buttons or low-contrast screens. That is why we designed the interface with large buttons, high contrast options, and the possibility of audio help.
9. Who is your client (the group paying for the solution)?
We see the main potential clients as the transport operators (like MTR and bus companies), the Transport Department, and NGOs that work with disabled people. These organisations might want to support or use a tool that makes public transport more inclusive and reduces complaints about accessibility.
10. How did you gather information about the needs and preferences of your target audience?
At this stage, our understanding comes mainly from secondary research and team review of the prototype. We have tested the technical workflow with Hong Kong place names, including a route from Kowloon Tong to Hong Kong Baptist Hospital, but this is not a substitute for user research. We do not yet have results from formal accessibility testing or interviews with wheelchair users and older adults; gathering that feedback is a planned next step.
11. How is your team structured, and what roles does each member play?
We are a team of five students from HKU and CityU. Our work is divided across product planning, accessible interface design, frontend and map implementation, backend and data integration, and testing and presentation preparation. The current prototype has working frontend-to-backend route and slope analysis; broader scoring and accessibility validation remain under development.
12. What communication strategies are you using to ensure effective collaboration?
We mostly use GitHub to share code and leave comments, and we message each other regularly for quick updates. We also have short weekly meetings to discuss bigger decisions. This mix of online updates and face-to-face (or online) discussions has helped us stay on the same page.
13. What have you achieved so far?
We have built a working local prototype with a React/Vite frontend and a Flask backend. Users can search for Hong Kong place names, request a Mapbox walking route, view slope-coloured route segments based on a local DEM, and see nearby facility markers and summary information. In an end-to-end test from Kowloon Tong to Hong Kong Baptist Hospital, the API returned a route and facility data in about 11 seconds. The backend route tests pass, and the frontend lint and production build pass. We have not built a route scoring system or published this version as a GitHub Pages demo.
14. What part of your project are you currently working on?
The current focus is improving the reliability and usefulness of the route workflow: checking geocoded place results, measuring slope-sampling performance, and documenting setup and known limitations. The prototype currently calculates one walking route at a time. Obstacle reporting, audio support, and formal accessibility scoring have not been implemented yet.
15. What are your immediate next steps or milestones?
Our next steps are to validate route and slope results against real Hong Kong conditions, improve location and facility data quality, obtain a valid BarrierFreeMap API key if that data source is to be used, and conduct usability testing with wheelchair users and older adults. We also need to make fallback routes unmistakable, define how route risks should be presented, and prepare a demo that clearly distinguishes working features from planned ones.
16. How will you evaluate the success of your project as it progresses?
We will evaluate route and geocoding accuracy against known Hong Kong locations, compare DEM slope estimates with observed route conditions, measure response time and facility coverage, and check the interface against basic accessibility criteria. Once user testing is possible, we will also measure task completion and collect feedback on whether the map and slope colours help users understand route conditions. The current prototype has not yet been evaluated through a formal user study.
17. What challenges or obstacles have you encountered so far? How did you address these challenges?
We encountered slow route calculations because the DEM sampling code repeatedly read the full terrain raster for individual samples. We changed it to use Rasterio sampling and aligned the sample interval with the DEM's 5-metre resolution. We also corrected the longitude/latitude order sent to Mapbox Directions and changed place search after testing showed that the previous geocoder returned nearby roads instead of the intended Hong Kong POIs. The end-to-end test now completes in about 11 seconds, although external data services can still fail or return incomplete results.
18. Do you foresee any potential challenges in the upcoming stages of your project? How do you intend to overcome these challenges?
The main risks are incomplete or inaccurate map and facility data, DEM values that do not capture sidewalks or temporary barriers, external API outages, and the possibility that users mistake a walking route for a verified accessible route. We will label limitations clearly, test routes against local observations, seek user feedback, and avoid presenting an accessibility score as validated until we have evidence to support it.
19. What resources have you utilised so far in your project? How have these resources contributed to your progress?
The current implementation uses Python and Flask for the API, React and Vite for the frontend, Mapbox for the basemap and walking directions, Photon/OpenStreetMap for place-name geocoding, a local Hong Kong DEM for elevation sampling, and Overpass/OpenStreetMap for facility lookup. These resources support a working route-analysis prototype. BarrierFreeMap integration is present but currently requires a valid API key; its data is not available in the tested configuration.
20. Are there any resources that you need but currently lack?
We still need a valid BarrierFreeMap API key if we want to use that source, more verified facility and pedestrian-route data, and access to wheelchair users and older adults for usability testing. We also lack official real-time lift, bus, and temporary-obstacle data. These features are not currently simulated in the prototype.
21. What have you learned so far?
We have learned that a route can be technically generated without being safe or accessible, so results need clear limitations and real-world validation. Separating the React frontend from the Flask API makes it easier to change the interface and route processing independently. We also found that data-source failures and inefficient raster access can strongly affect the user experience, so performance checks and graceful error handling are part of the product work, not just implementation details.
22. How has your understanding of the problem or solution evolved since you started working on it?
Our focus has shifted from describing a broad all-in-one accessibility platform to proving a smaller technical workflow: place search, walking-route retrieval, terrain-slope analysis, and facility display. Testing revealed important limits, including geocoder accuracy, dependence on external services, and the difference between terrain slope and true step-free accessibility. We are now treating risk communication and validation as priorities before expanding the feature set.
23. What other issues or comments does your team want to raise?
NAVI is intended to help people with limited mobility make better-informed journey decisions, but the current prototype is not a certified accessibility-navigation service. It displays walking routes, terrain-derived slope information, and available facility data; it does not verify every route as wheelchair-accessible or provide live operational status. We welcome feedback from disability groups and target users to guide data validation, presentation of risks, and future testing.
